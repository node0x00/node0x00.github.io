Design report

Project Qwen

An offline home-security and assistant robot, built on salvaged hoverboard motors, that sees, listens, guards the house and docks itself to recharge.

Hugo Richmond · Computer Engineering July 7 – October 17, 2026
Abstract

Qwen is a home robot I designed and built from scratch over about three months. A Raspberry Pi 4 (4 GB) runs everything that needs intelligence, all offline: a custom wake word, speech recognition, a local language model, text-to-speech, hand-gesture control, owner face recognition and person detection. An ESP32 handles everything that needs real-time timing: closed-loop wheel speed control on salvaged hoverboard hub motors, obstacle reflexes, the scissor-lift servos and status LEDs. In security mode the robot sleeps on a motion sensor, wakes to check faces, warns and records intruders, sounds an alarm and sends alerts to my phone. It returns to its charger by itself: it finds two AprilTags on the wall, lines up on wheel odometry, turns around and reverses onto spring contacts, and confirms the dock by measuring charging current. If it can't see the tags, it falls back to monocular ORB-SLAM to search the room. This report covers the design, the math, what failed and how each problem was solved.

01

Goals and constraints

I wanted a robot that is actually useful around the house rather than a demo: something I can talk to, that keeps an eye on the place while I'm out, and that looks after its own battery. I organized the plan as a feature checklist and kept it honest as the project went, so the final status of every item is in section 15.

AreaPlanned features
AI interactionLocal LLM, wake word ("Hey Qwen"), voice commands, spoken answers, owner face recognition, general Q&A
VisionVideo and photos, hand gestures, face recognition, object and person detection
SecurityMotion detection, unknown-face response, recording, alarm, flashlight, phone alerts
NavigationObstacle avoidance, return to dock, automatic recharging, room mapping
PersonalityIdle animations, smooth "neck" motion on the scissor lift

Constraints that drove every decision:

  • Compute. A Raspberry Pi 4 with 4 GB of RAM and no GPU. RAM, not storage, was the binding limit.
  • Power. A salvaged hoverboard Li-ion pack, 25.2 V nominal (7 cells in series). The robot should live on it for long stretches and recharge itself.
  • Offline. No cloud services for speech, language or vision. Only alerts leave the house.
  • Budget. Mostly salvaged and second-hand parts (section 17).

Guiding principle: only cheap processes run all the time, and heavy work happens on demand. That rule decided the wake-word design, the security power states, the idle mode and how the camera is shared.

Board split. The Pi does perception and decisions; the ESP32 does anything with a deadline. Safety reflexes such as obstacle stops and the motor watchdog live on the ESP32, so they never depend on a round trip to the Pi.

02

Design and build timeline

Qwen is an original design. I started with hand sketches of the body and head, modeled them in Fusion 360 and printed every part in PETG. The one borrowed mechanism is the scissor lift, which I adapted from a design on Thingiverse.

  1. Month 1
    Jul 7 –

    AI and vision first. Voice pipeline, custom wake word, gestures and the camera layer, developed on my laptop with the same webcam. In parallel I started reverse engineering the hoverboard's mainboard over UART, researched parts and bench-tested components one at a time.

  2. Month 2

    Mechanics. Tore down the hoverboard and wired the hub motors to separate BLDC controllers (with several failures on the way, section 7). Built the scissor lift, which immediately ran into a weight limit: the servos could not raise the head with a stock webcam, so I stripped the Logitech C920 out of its housing. Designed and printed the lower body and head, mounted the wheels and motors, and laid out all the wiring on perfboard.

  3. Month 3
    – Oct 17

    Integration. Security mode and the smart-home link, soldering everything onto perfboards, wheel odometry, closed-loop speed control, docking, power management, and merging every subsystem into one program that starts at boot.

03

System architecture

Everything on the Pi runs in one Python process (assistant.py) started by a systemd service at boot and restarted automatically if it crashes. One process means one owner for each shared resource: one camera manager, one serial link to the ESP32, one web server. That turned out to matter more than any single algorithm (section 13). The only separate program is a small C++ tag tracker, which borrows the camera while the robot drives itself.

Pi–ESP32 protocol rules, enforced on the ESP32:

  • A 500 ms watchdog stops the motors if the Pi goes silent.
  • A blocked IR sensor stops forward motion without involving the Pi.
  • Reverse is limited by distance (450 mm measured by odometry), because the robot has no rear sensors.
  • The manual potentiometer takes over the lift only when it is physically turned.
04

Voice assistant

First prototype, and why I replaced it

The first pipeline avoided training a wake word: a voice-activity detector cut speech into utterances, Whisper transcribed every one, and the text was fuzzy-matched against "hey qwen". It worked, but it ran speech recognition almost continuously, which breaks the guiding principle on a board that also has to run vision. I replaced it before it ever reached the Pi.

Final pipeline

StageChoiceWhy
Wake wordopenWakeWord, custom modelA tiny always-on classifier; everything heavier runs only after it fires
Speech to textfaster-whisper tiny.en, int8Smallest model that understood me reliably on the Pi's CPU
Language modelOllama, qwen2.5:1.5bFits in RAM next to vision; kept resident for fast answers
Text to speechPiper, en_US-lessac-mediumNatural voice, runs locally
Command routingFuzzy intent matcherKnown commands skip the LLM entirely

I originally set the LLM to unload after 3 idle minutes to free about 1 GB. On the robot the reload delay made the first answer feel broken, and with everything running the Pi still had about 1.7 GB available (measured with free -m), so it now stays loaded.

The speaker sits a few centimetres from the microphone, so the robot could wake itself. The microphone is muted while it speaks plus a 0.3 s tail:

self.mic.mute_for(duration_s + MIC_MUTE_TAIL_S)   # tail = 0.3 s, so its own voice can't trigger "Hey Qwen"

Training the wake word

I trained the wake word with openWakeWord's notebook, which generates synthetic speech so no recordings are needed. Its text-to-speech read "Qwen" as the letter Q followed by "wen"; respelling the phrase "hey kwen" fixed the pronunciation. A later ImportError came from a half-upgraded numpy/scipy pair and went away with a clean runtime. The result is a 206 KB ONNX model with a detection threshold of 0.30 on the robot.

Voice commands

Start/stop recording, take a photo, flashlight on/off, look up/down (lift), turn, move forward/back, "what do you see" (object detection read aloud), "is anyone here", "security mode" and "alarm". Anything else goes to the language model. I checked the router offline against 13 phrasings, including commands buried in longer sentences; all 13 routed correctly and the unrelated questions fell through to the LLM.

Development-environment failures

Most early failures were environment problems, each fixed one layer at a time: Linux setup steps run on macOS, Python's protected system install (fixed with a virtual environment), setuptools 81 removing pkg_resources (pinned below 81), a Piper API change, and a shell alias that pointed python at a different interpreter than pip. On the Pi itself MediaPipe had no wheel for Python 3.13, so the robot runs a separate Python 3.12 environment.

05

Vision: gestures, faces and objects

Hand gestures

MediaPipe's hand landmarker returns 21 points per hand. Instead of training a gesture model, I classify gestures with geometric rules on those points, which is cheap enough to run continuously at 12 fps. Every distance is divided by the palm size (wrist to middle knuckle), so the rules don't depend on how far the hand is from the camera. A gesture has to hold for 0.15 s, and there's a 2 s cooldown after each action.

Telling a fist from a thumbs-up took four iterations. A live overlay that drew the skeleton and printed the raw metrics turned each one into a measurement instead of a guess:

#Thumb ruleWhat went wrongWhy
1Thumb tip farther from the wrist than the thumb baseFist read as thumbs-upA thumb tucked over curled fingers is still far from the wrist
2Thumb tip > 0.75 palm from the index knuckleOpen hand read as unknownMeasured 0.35 with the thumb alongside the palm: it measured spread, not straightness
3Thumb straightness (cosine at the middle joint) > 0.7Fist still mostly thumbs-upMy fist keeps the thumb straight, laid across the fingers
4Fingers curled and thumb tip above the folded fingertipsWorksUses height, not shape; threshold tuned to 1.25 palm
\[ r_{\text{thumb}} = \frac{\bar{y}_{\text{folded tips}} - y_{\text{thumb tip}}}{\lVert p_{\text{wrist}} - p_{\text{middle MCP}} \rVert} \;>\; 1.25 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{thumbs up} \]

Rather than keep tuning, I also changed the vocabulary so no two commands depend on that fist/thumbs-up boundary:

GestureAction
Thumbs up (not recording)Start recording
OK sign (while recording)Stop recording — thumb and index tips within 0.4 palm, other fingers extended
Peace signPhoto after a 1.5 s delay
Rock sign, held 1 sToggle the flashlight

Gestures need light; in the dark the hand isn't detected, so voice is the main way to switch the flashlight on.

Owner face recognition

Faces are found with YuNet and compared with SFace embeddings (both OpenCV models). There is one enrolled owner; every other face is "unknown", and several faces are classified independently. A face counts as the owner when the cosine similarity to the stored embedding is at least 0.363. Recognition turned out to depend on conditions (lift height, room lights), so I enroll the owner once per condition and keep all the embeddings.

Objects and people

Object and person detection uses YOLO26n exported to NCNN, which runs well on the Pi's ARM cores: a 320-pixel model for continuous person checks in security mode and a 640-pixel model when I ask "what do you see". A live debug view in the browser draws faces, objects and hands on the camera feed.

06

Security mode

Security mode is a state machine built around power: the expensive parts only run when there is a reason to.

  • Arming. "Hey Qwen, security mode", or the exit button on my keypad. A 100 s exit delay lets me leave.
  • Armed-idle. Camera, microphone, gestures and room lights off. Only the PIR motion sensor is watched.
  • Armed-active. Motion turns on the room lights (through the smart-home hub) and the camera, and starts face recognition plus person detection at 2 fps. Two minutes with no face or person returns to armed-idle, treating the wake-up as a false trigger.

Intruder response

An unknown face (seen for 1.5 s and at least 110 px wide, so tiny distant faces don't count) starts an incident: the robot records video and says "You are trespassing and being recorded, leave the premises immediately." If the intruder is still there after 25 s, a looping alarm starts (capped at 5 minutes). The intruder counts as present while either an unknown face or a person shape is seen. In testing, tying it to the face alone ended the incident whenever the person turned away, and the alarm never fired.

Disarming and remote unlock

Qwen plugs into my ESP32 smart-home system (door lock, keypad and light switches on an ESP-NOW hub, which is a separate project). I designed two ways to disarm: the owner's face, or the correct keypad code. From the hub's web page I can also unlock the door remotely for someone. That switches security mode on in any state, raises the lift to 130° so the camera can see faces from the floor, and gives 3 minutes to verify the owner's face or a keypad code before the alarm sounds. Recognizing the owner cancels the keypad countdown and locks the door again.

Every step is reported on Telegram: motion photos, unknown-person photos, recordings, remote-unlock status. Media files are deleted from the Pi once delivered.

07

Drivetrain: from hoverboard to closed-loop control

I wanted to reuse the salvaged hoverboard's hub motors. My first plan was to keep its mainboard and command it from the ESP32.

Identifying the controller

I first tried to program the mainboard with an ST-Link over its SWD header. The usual STM32 tools kept failing to recognize the chip, which made me look closer: it is a Linkosemi LKS32MC088C6T8 (Cortex-M0), not an STM32. The popular open-source hoverboard firmwares don't support it, and writing motor-control firmware for an undocumented chip was out of scope.

Reverse engineering the board-to-board link

Next I tried leaving the stock firmware in place and speaking its protocol. I tapped the link between the two boards (12, R1, T1, G, 3.3 V logic). While probing, a charged capacitor bank held 28 V with the battery unplugged and caused a spark, so I discharge the bus before probing now. ESP32-based sniffing gave readable bytes, but copying them from screenshots introduced errors (the letter O for zero), so I switched to a USB logic analyzer with PulseView.

\[ T_{\text{bit}} = 13\ \mu\text{s} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{baud} = \frac{1}{13\ \mu\text{s}} \approx 76\,923 \text{ bit/s (8N1)} \]

The capture showed fixed 19-byte frames: a constant idle frame (55 00 00 … 34 D8) and an active frame (55 CB FF …) whose last bytes changed with the payload, consistent with a checksum (hypothesis). I generated C arrays straight from the decoder export and replayed them from the ESP32 into the right board, with the original wire cut to avoid bus contention.

Negative result

With valid traffic replayed continuously, both boards stayed powered with no fault, but the wheels never moved and the board still reacted to tilt. My interpretation (hypothesis): the link carries wheel-to-wheel telemetry, while the balance loop and motor commands are internal to each board. Proving it would mean disassembling the stock firmware, so I chose to replace the controller instead.

Replacement controllers (ZS-X11H)

The first ZS-X11H locked the motor and vibrated; about 30 s into the stall it started smoking. That fits a stalled-rotor current from a hall/phase mismatch (hypothesis). Even on a 24 V, 5 A bench supply none of the 36 hall-order combinations ran reliably. The root cause is still unknown. A second controller worked once I bridged the pads that enable PWM input and wired its P pin.

An Arduino could drive it, but the ESP32 couldn't, because its 3.3 V logic high was below the controller's input threshold. A bidirectional 3.3 V–5 V level shifter fixed it. Each motor now uses only two signals, DIR and PWM.

Wheel odometry

The robot needed to know how far each wheel had turned. The hub motors already contain hall sensors, so I tapped two of them (A and B) on each motor controller's hall connector and fed them to the ESP32. The halls switch at 5 V and the ESP32 tolerates 3.3 V, so each tap goes through a resistor divider made of three 10 kΩ resistors: one in series, two in series to ground.

\[ V_{\text{GPIO}} = V_{\text{hall}}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = 5\ \text{V}\cdot\frac{20\ \text{k}\Omega}{10\ \text{k}\Omega + 20\ \text{k}\Omega} = 3.33\ \text{V}, \qquad I = \frac{5\ \text{V}}{30\ \text{k}\Omega} \approx 0.17\ \text{mA} \]

Each valid change in the two-bit hall state is one count with a known direction; jumps of two states (noise) count as zero, so a glitch cancels out instead of drifting. I measured 60 counts per wheel revolution. With the 6.5-inch (162 mm) wheels:

\[ C = \pi d = \pi \cdot 162\ \text{mm} \approx 509\ \text{mm}, \qquad k = \frac{C}{N} = \frac{509\ \text{mm}}{60} \approx 8.48\ \text{mm/count} \]

Driving measured distances on the floor gave k = 8.5 mm/count, matching the geometry. For a differential drive with wheel spacing b, each update moves the robot by

\[ \Delta s = \frac{k(\Delta n_L + \Delta n_R)}{2}, \qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{k(\Delta n_R - \Delta n_L)}{b}, \qquad \begin{aligned} x &\mathrel{+}= \Delta s\cos\!\left(\theta + \tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right)\\ y &\mathrel{+}= \Delta s\sin\!\left(\theta + \tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right)\end{aligned} \]

Turning in place on my wooden floor slips: a commanded 180° turn on the measured spacing came out near 200°. Calibrating against the camera gave an effective spacing of b = 365 mm. The resolution is coarse, which shaped the docking design later:

\[ \Delta\theta_{\min} = \frac{k}{b} = \frac{8.5}{365}\ \text{rad} \approx 1.3^\circ \text{ per count of one wheel} \]

Closed-loop speed control

Open-loop PWM made "slow" mean different speeds at different battery voltages, and the two wheels never matched. On the floor the robot also needed speeds well below what the controller can hold open-loop. So the ESP32 runs a 50 Hz speed loop per wheel. Speed comes from the time between hall counts, measured over two hall steps:

\[ \hat v = \frac{2k}{\Delta t_2}, \qquad u = K_{ff}\, v^{*} + K_p\, e + K_i \!\int e\,dt, \qquad e = v^{*} - \hat v \]

with \(K_{ff} = 0.025\), \(K_p = 0.008\) and \(K_i = 0.06\) (output in drive units per mm/s, integrator clamped). The feed-forward term comes from measuring about 40 mm/s per drive unit with the wheels lifted. Two more terms made it behave on the floor:

  • Acceleration limit of 200 mm/s². Hard starts lifted the rear caster and slammed it down. The Pi uses the same number to start braking early: \( s_b = \dfrac{v^2}{2a} \), which is only 2.3 mm at 30 mm/s but 42 mm at 130 mm/s.
  • Wheel sync for straight driving and spins: each wheel's target is corrected by \( K_s\,(n_L - n_R) \) with \(K_s = 12\) mm/s per count, so one wheel can't run ahead.

The lower limit is physics. At 20 mm/s a wheel produces a count only every \( k / v = 8.5 / 20 \approx 0.43 \) s, so the controller sees very little feedback; that's why the slowest manual speeds still look slightly uneven. In practice the robot drives anywhere from 6 mm/s (fine turning) to 130 mm/s.

Power distribution

The INA226 on its own charging branch

My first plan put the INA226's shunt in series with the main battery lead to measure total current. That can't work: hub-motor current is far beyond what the breakout's shunt can measure.

\[ I_{\max} = \frac{V_{\text{shunt,max}}}{R_{\text{shunt}}} = \frac{81.92\ \text{mV}}{0.1\ \Omega} = 0.819\ \text{A} \]

What the robot really needs to know is whether it is charging, so the INA226 sits on a separate charging branch: IN+ to the battery, IN− toward the charging contact, with the bus-voltage pin tied to IN− so the same chip also reports pack voltage. Charging current reads negative in this orientation; I confirmed the sign on the robot and flip it in software.

Finding

While charging, the INA226 reports exactly 0.819 A every time. That is the full-scale value above, so the real charge current is at least 0.82 A and the sensor is saturated. It still answers "charging or not" reliably, which is all the docking logic needs, but measuring the true current needs a smaller shunt (section 16). The original shunt also burned out once and had to be replaced.

Battery percentage. Li-ion voltage isn't linear in charge, so I divide the pack voltage by the 7 cells and interpolate a per-cell discharge curve (3.20 V = 0%, 3.70 V = 47%, 4.20 V = 100%, eleven points):

\[ v = \frac{V_{\text{pack}}}{7}, \qquad \%(v) = p_i + (p_{i+1} - p_i)\,\frac{v - v_i}{v_{i+1} - v_i}, \quad v_i \le v \le v_{i+1} \]

Charger and diode. The charger measures 29.7 V with no load on my multimeter. The 1N4007 in the positive pad lead drops roughly 0.8 V, so the pack tops out near 28.9 V:

\[ \frac{28.9\ \text{V}}{7} \approx 4.13\ \text{V/cell} \;\Rightarrow\; \approx 94\% \text{ on the curve} \]

That is gentler on the cells than a full 4.2 V and still above the 90% point where the robot drives off the charger (section 12).

09

Docking and automatic recharging

The dock is deliberately simple: two spring-loaded metal pads, the same kind robot vacuums use, glued to a flat plastic plate on the wall just above the baseboard. The robot's contacts are on its back, and it has no rear sensors, so docking means reversing blind onto two small pads. Above the dock are two printed AprilTags (tag36h11, 79 mm black square, IDs 0 and 1, 160 mm apart).

Plan versus outcome

Question at planning timeWhat I found
Can ORB-SLAM run on a Pi 4 alongside everything else?Yes, at about 5–10 fps (70–160 ms per frame) with the Pi reaching about 75 °C. Too slow and too heavy for docking, so docking uses AprilTags, and ORB-SLAM became the lost-tag fallback (section 10).
What gives scale and odometry without an IMU?Hall-sensor wheel odometry (section 7) for distance, and the tags for metric position near the dock.
Forward or reverse onto the contacts?Approach forward facing the tags, turn 180°, then reverse using a "docked view" match from the camera, now facing the room.
How is a successful dock confirmed?Charging current on the INA226 branch, held for 3 s.

Pose from the tags

The camera (calibrated focal length \(f = 645\) px) gives the tag's rotation \(R\) and translation \(t\). Inverting that gives the robot's position in the wall's frame: lateral (sideways from tag 0, + to the right when facing the wall), out (distance from the wall) and heading:

\[ p = -R^{\mathsf T} t, \qquad \text{lateral} = p_x,\quad \text{out} = p_z, \qquad f = R^{\mathsf T}\!\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad \text{heading} = \operatorname{atan2}(f_x,\,-f_z) \]

One pixel is \(1/f \approx 0.089^\circ\), and a 79 mm tag at 1 m is only \(79 \cdot 645 / 1000 \approx 51\) px wide, so every source of blur mattered.

The four steps

  1. Find and line up. A 360° stop-and-look scan finds the tags. Farther than 95 cm out, the robot first drives straight at them to about 80 cm, then sidesteps (turn 90°, drive the offset on odometry, turn back) until it is within 1.5 cm of the dock line after facing the wall again.
  2. Drive to the turn spot in 10 cm odometry legs, re-facing the wall between legs, and stop 37 cm out. If it arrives more than 3 cm off the line, it backs out to 70 cm and lines up again.
  3. Turn around. Turning in place slips on this floor, so the turn is checked by the camera: it spins in small speed-controlled chunks until the view matches the "docked view" taught earlier.
  4. Back on. It compares ORB features in the live image against pictures taken while docked to keep its heading within 3°, then reverses in gentle 25 mm/s pulses. It stops the moment charging current appears, and the dock counts only when charging holds for 3 s.

Calibrating the dock line from manual docks

My first "dock line" (the lateral position the robot should hold) was computed from the geometry, and the robot kept landing a few centimetres off. So I drove five docks by hand while the robot recorded where I was before turning around. The lateral positions were 8.6, 8.6, 10.0, 9.4 and 8.5 cm; the turn distances were 32, 38, 38, 41 and 36 cm.

\[ \bar{x} = 9.02\ \text{cm},\quad s_x = 0.66\ \text{cm} \;\Rightarrow\; x_{\text{line}} = 9.0\ \text{cm},\ \text{tolerance } \pm 1.5\ \text{cm} \ (\approx 2.3\,s_x); \qquad \bar{d} = 37.0\ \text{cm} \]

Small heading errors during the blind reverse grow into sideways error at the pads:

\[ e_{\text{pads}} \approx e_{\text{line}} + L\sin\psi, \qquad L = 37\ \text{cm},\ \psi = 1^\circ \;\Rightarrow\; L\sin\psi \approx 6.5\ \text{mm} \]

That's why the heading is re-checked against the docked view every few centimetres while reversing, and why the line tolerance is tight.

10

Room mapping and the lost-tag search

Docking only works when the robot can see the tags. If the 360° scan finds nothing (it was carried to another room, or the tags are behind furniture), Qwen switches the tag tracker into monocular ORB-SLAM mode and searches. It drives slowly outward in gentle arcs, with the IR sensors stopping it for obstacles, and builds a map as it goes. The map lets it steer toward directions it hasn't covered yet. Every metre or so it stops and repeats the tag scan. As soon as either tag is seen, normal docking takes over; if the search runs out of time, it tells me on Telegram.

Running ORB-SLAM on a Pi 4

I built ORB-SLAM3 on the Pi and wrapped it in a C++ program that also does the AprilTag detection, so the two share one camera stream. With 800 features per frame, tracking takes 70–160 ms per frame (5–10 fps). Two numbers shaped how the robot has to drive in this mode. A monocular camera can't measure depth from rotation alone, and fast turns move features too far between frames:

\[ \Delta\phi_{\text{frame}} = \frac{\omega}{f_{\text{fps}}}: \qquad \frac{41^\circ/\text{s}}{5.5\ \text{fps}} \approx 7.5^\circ \text{ per frame (tracking lost)}, \qquad \frac{9^\circ/\text{s}}{5.5\ \text{fps}} \approx 1.6^\circ \text{ per frame} \]

So in SLAM mode turns are limited to about 9°/s and done as arcs, never pivots.

Building a 2D floor map

To test mapping I drove the robot around the room by hand while it recorded every frame plus wheel counts and tag sightings, then processed the recording offline. ORB-SLAM gives a sparse 3D point cloud, not a floor plan, so I built the 2D map myself:

  • The floor plane comes from the camera's "up" direction, averaged over all keyframes.
  • Points between 3 cm and 60 cm above the floor are obstacles; rays from each keyframe to what it saw mark free space.
  • ORB-SLAM starts a new map every time it loses tracking, so the wheel odometry is the backbone: every map piece is placed on the odometry path with a rotation, a scale and an offset.
\[ Q \approx s\,R(\alpha)\,S + t, \qquad s = \frac{\sum \lVert \Delta Q \rVert}{\sum \lVert \Delta S \rVert}, \qquad \alpha = \arg\!\Big(\sum e^{\,i(\theta_{\text{odo}} - \psi_{\text{slam}})}\Big) \]

where \(S\) is a SLAM piece's path in the floor plane, \(Q\) the odometry path at the same times, \(\theta\) and \(\psi\) the two headings, and \(t\) the mean remaining offset.

Limits I measured

The map shows the room's main structure but is not accurate enough for path planning. The weak link is heading: across the second drive the robot turned a total of 8,474°, and every spin on wood slips a little. By the end, the dock's position estimated from different tag sightings disagreed by about 1.3 m. One camera also has no true scale, so each SLAM piece's scale wanders. That's why the map is used to search (where have I been?) and not to navigate, and why docking itself relies on the tags. A gyro would fix the heading drift (section 16).

Power management and idle mode

Battery behaviour

  • LEDs, solid at every level, charging or not: red at 25% or below, yellow 26–50%, green above 50%.
  • Low battery. At 25% or less, not charging, and idle for 30 s (so motor current sag can't trigger it), the robot says "Low battery, returning to dock" and runs the four docking steps, retrying up to three times before alerting me.
  • Full. "90%" is a charging-voltage threshold, because the charger lifts the measured voltage: 28.75 V held for 60 s while charging (about 28.55 V at rest). Then it says "Battery full" and drives 13 cm forward off the pads.
  • No servo motion while charging. The lift servos' current pulled the contacts' voltage around and disrupted charging, so the lift is locked on the dock. Security mode is the one exception, because the remote-unlock face check needs the camera up.

Idle mode

Following the guiding principle, if nothing moves for two minutes, Qwen switches off the camera, gesture tracking and the microphone and leaves only the motion sensor on. Any motion brings everything back. Talking to it, driving, recording, security mode or someone watching the live view keep it awake. The language model stays loaded so the first answer after waking is instant, and the battery manager keeps running so low-battery docking still works.

Estimated running cost

I haven't measured the robot's average draw yet, so this is an estimate from typical figures for its parts: about 4–5 W asleep in security mode, 9–11 W while actively watching, and short driving bursts. That averages about 6 W at the battery, or about 7.5 W from the wall after charger and battery losses.

\[ E = 7.5\ \text{W} \times 24\ \text{h} = 0.18\ \text{kWh/day}, \qquad 0.18 \times 30.4 \times \$0.1338 \approx \$0.73\ \text{per month} \]

At my utility's rate that's under $9 a year for a robot that runs around the clock.

Known hazard

If the ESP32 loses power or resets, the level shifter's pull-ups hold both PWM inputs at 5 V and the motors run at full speed. Until the pull-down fix is fitted, I never shut down the Pi or reflash the ESP32 with the wheels on the ground.

How I used AI

I used AI assistants throughout this project, mainly to speed up debugging. Typical uses: reading logs and measurements with me to narrow a fault down faster, drafting code for a subsystem I had specified, and checking datasheets and pin maps.

The decisions, the hardware and the testing were mine. I set the requirements and made the design calls. I built, wired and soldered the robot, drove every test and judged the results on the real hardware. I also caught the AI's mistakes: one AI-drafted revision of the security specification added a requirement I had never asked for, and several fixes it suggested were wrong until I measured what the robot actually did. AI-generated specifications and code need the same review as anyone else's. The most useful habit I built was writing self-contained handoff documents that restate the constraints and the decisions already made, so settled questions don't get re-argued.

Future improvements

Safety first

  • Fuses. A main fuse right after the battery and a fuse on the INA226 charging branch. Neither is fitted yet. Fuses belong on the supply side, never in the ground lead.
  • Protect the Pi's 5 V. A 3 A resettable PTC fuse and a 5 V TVS diode, because header power bypasses the Pi's own input protection.
  • 10 kΩ pull-downs on both PWM lines (or a controller enable line), so the motors stop when the ESP32 isn't driving them. This fixes the known hazard in section 13.
  • An emergency-stop switch in the motor supply.

Sensing and navigation

  • A gyro (IMU) such as a BNO085 for heading that doesn't drift with wheel slip. This is the single change that would most improve the map, the turns and the docking.
  • A smaller shunt (0.01 Ω) on the INA226 to measure the real charging current instead of saturating.
  • A 2D lidar for a navigation-grade map.
  • A dock funnel or wider pads, so a few millimetres of error still lands on the contacts.

Other

  • A fan on the Pi's heatsink: with vision running it reaches the low 80s °C and briefly throttles.
  • Measure real power draw, and the docking success rate over a long series of runs.
  • Scheduled patrol routes, once the map is trustworthy.

What I learned

  • Resource-aware design works. Cheap things always on, heavy things on demand, made an offline voice and vision robot fit on a 4 GB board.
  • Measure before tuning. The gesture overlay, the logic analyzer and the docking log each turned guessing into measuring.
  • Change the problem when tuning stalls. A new gesture vocabulary beat a fifth threshold; a second tag beat clever single-tag logic; calibrating from my own manual docks beat the geometry.
  • Negative results are results. The hoverboard replay and the mapping drift each pointed to the right next decision.
  • Electrical decisions come from failure modes, not ratings: fuse placement, strapping pins, level shifter pull-ups and shunt range all bit me or nearly did.
  • Integration is its own engineering problem. One owner per shared resource fixed a whole class of bugs at once.
